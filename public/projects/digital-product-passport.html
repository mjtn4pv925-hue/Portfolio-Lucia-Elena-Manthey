Beyond Compliance

How the EU's Digital Product Passport can turn fashion transparency into communication people trust.

Context
Master's thesis, International Entrepreneurship, Digitalization & Sustainability, Neu-Ulm University of Applied Sciences, 2026
Grade
1.0 – selected for publication in an edited volume
Methods
10 expert interviews, consumer-journey framework (AISDALSLove), thematic analysis
Focus
Sustainability communication, circular economy, fashion

The challenge

Fashion is one of the most polluting industries, and its green claims are met with growing distrust. In response, the EU is introducing the Digital Product Passport (DPP): sustainability information at product level, usually accessed through a QR code. A simplified version for textiles is planned from 2027.

Most brands see the DPP as a compliance task. I wanted to know: how can it become a communication tool that builds trust and helps people act more circularly?

What I did

  • Talked to the people shaping it. I interviewed 10 experts from across the fashion ecosystem: a premium retailer, a luxury fashion brand, a sustainable-first brand that already uses a DPP, a multi-brand e-commerce retailer, a small sustainable label, a circular business, a sustainability consultancy, a traceability technology provider, a certification and standards body, and a circular economy policy expert.
  • Followed the consumer journey. Every interview was structured along the AISDALSLove model, from first attention to purchase, sharing and loyalty, to see where the DPP actually makes a difference.
  • Found the patterns. I coded and analysed all interviews with thematic analysis to identify where the DPP adds value, and where it doesn't.

Key insights

  1. Infrastructure, not marketing.

    The Digital Product Passport is not a marketing tool that directly drives behaviour, but a communicative infrastructure that reshapes how sustainability is evaluated.

  2. Its impact is conditional, not inherent.

    Transparency only creates value when it is actively designed, translated and embedded into consumer touchpoints.

  3. Trust over persuasion.

    Instead of persuading consumers, the DPP mainly supports trust formation, evaluation and sense-making through verifiable, product-level information.

  4. An enabler for circularity.

    It does not directly drive circular behaviour, but can enable it by reducing complexity and making options like repair or resale more accessible.

“It builds trust in us as a brand, because we openly share the information we have and show that we actually do what we claim.”

Impact Innovation Director, sustainable-first fashion brand

What this means for brands

The question is no longer whether to implement the DPP, but how to translate regulatory transparency into communication people understand and use. That means simple first layers with more depth on demand, active pointers to the passport in stores, online and through staff, and circular services built right into it.

Behind the analysis

From 80+ initial codes to 20 categories and five core themes.
